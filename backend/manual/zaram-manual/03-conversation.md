# Talking to Zaram

## Opening and closing the conversation

Click the orb — the sphere in the middle of the landing screen — to open the conversation. The orb slides to the left and the conversation opens beside it. Click the orb again, or press **Escape**, to close it and return to the landing.

The keyboard shortcut **Shift+Space** opens the conversation from anywhere in Zaram.

## Asking

![The conversation open beside the orb: the composer at the bottom with attach, microphone and send; the past-conversations tab at the left edge.](assets/conversation.png)

Type in the box at the bottom and press **Enter**. Shift+Enter makes a new line. While Zaram is answering, the orb shows it is working, and the line under the orb says what it is doing — recalling facts, reading a file, drawing.

## What a reply shows you

Under every reply is a small line that says which model answered and where it ran — *on this machine* or *left this device* — and, if Zaram remembered something to answer it, which facts and documents it used. Click a citation to see the source. If a recalled fact is wrong, correct it there and the next answer changes.

The same line says how long the first word took. Hover it for where the time went — recalling, planning, the model reading the conversation, writing.

## Watching it work

When Zaram uses a tool — searches your code, reads a file, looks something up on the web — a row appears in the reply where it happened, between the paragraphs, in the order it happened: *Searching the web…*, then *Searched the web · 4 results*. Rows fold to one line when they finish. Each row has **open**, which shows what that step did: what it read, what it changed (with **Revert**), and what it sent off this machine — or *"Nothing left this device for this step"*, which is a real answer, read from the log.

A model that can plan writes a checklist at the top of its reply and ticks it as it goes.

## Thinking

Some models think before they answer. The thought appears as one quiet line while it streams and folds away when the answer starts; click it to read the whole thing. **Thinking on/off** sits beside the routing choice at the bottom of the conversation. Off is much faster — a few tenths of a second instead of several seconds before the first word — and the answer is usually the same for ordinary questions. Turn it on for something hard.

## Naming a folder

Say where something is — *"have a look at C:\Work\northwind and tell me what add does"* — and Zaram offers to open that folder as a coding project, with one button. It then reads the folder with the code tools and asks your question again. Nothing is opened until you press the button.

## Attaching files and pictures

Click the paperclip, or drop a file onto the conversation, or paste. Documents are read on your machine. Pictures are read too: the text in a screenshot is read here by your computer's own text recogniser, and a model that can see gets the picture as well. Attachments belong to the message — they are not added to your memory unless you put them in Knowledge.

## Past conversations

At the left edge of the conversation is a thin tab. Hover or click it to see past conversations, grouped by day. Click one to resume it; Zaram picks up where it left off. **New** starts a fresh one.

## Revise and Remember

Under each reply:

- **Copy** copies the text.
- **Revise** lets you say how to change the reply — shorter, more formal, in French — and Zaram rewrites it.
- **Remember** keeps something from the reply in your memory on purpose. Zaram also remembers on its own as you go; this is the explicit version.

## When Zaram says it does not know

If Zaram cannot find what you are referring to, it says so and asks, rather than guessing. This is deliberate: a made-up answer in a document you send to a client is worse than a question. Give it the missing detail, or open the folder that holds it, and ask again.

## Notices

Sometimes a short line appears under a reply, in the colour of a footnote: the model's window filled up and it is answering from the last few exchanges; web search is off and the question looked like it needed it; a picture was drawn on a cloud provider because your graphics card was full; the tests failed twice and a stronger model is offered for this step. Each is one line, said once, and names what to do — often with the thing to do right there on the line, such as *Search the web and try again*.
